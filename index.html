To realise a requested manoeuvre, we need a trajectory that describes how the aircraft moves over time.

Our aerial robot testbed

Our testbed.
Variable-pitch model helicopter.

Three body rotations

Roll tilts side to side. Pitch tilts the nose up or down. Yaw turns the body around its vertical axis.

Variable blade pitch

Collective changes the pitch of both main rotor blades. Positive and negative pitch can reverse rotor thrust, enabling upside-down flight.

Together, these controls enable complex aerobatics, making this helicopter a useful testbed for complex aerial robot motion.

The motion space

A space of trajectories.
Only some are executable.

A helicopter trajectory

A trajectory describes how the helicopter’s state variables and control commands evolve over time.

  • State variables describe the helicopter’s position, velocity and body orientation.
  • Control commands are inputs from the pilot or controller, such as commands for blade pitch and body rotation.

Trajectory space T

The possible state-and-control histories form a high-dimensional trajectory space.

The feasible motion space

Dynamics and actuator limits restrict which trajectories the helicopter can physically execute.

Dynamic feasibility means that each next state follows from the current state and control command through the helicopter’s physical model.

xk+1 = f(xk, uk)
x: state · u: control command
f: one step of the physical model

Learning the feasible space

Learning a compact latent representation of this feasible trajectory space is demanding: the model must capture how motion variables interact while respecting the aircraft’s dynamics and physical limits.

The research question

A compact representation—
or a structured one?

Compression alone

A monolithic model can encode a complete movement in a compact latent representation.

But there are still potential drawbacks:

  • Latent coordinates may not clearly distinguish different sources of motion variation.
  • Rare or risky manoeuvres can still require many demonstrations that are difficult and costly to collect.
  • A monolithic model may need many parameters to capture complex motion, creating a large memory footprint for onboard deployment.

Our proposed factorization

We aim to learn a factorized latent representation that captures distinct sources of motion variation.

  • Physics-guided decomposition. Use physical invariances to identify separable motion factors.
  • Neural factor discovery. Learn a structured latent representation beyond the physics-derived decomposition, using component-specific encoders and decoders. Training will encourage distinct motion factors while preserving reconstruction fidelity.

Our hypothesis

Use learned structure
to generate new motion

Generate new combinations

Shared motion factors could be combined to generate a new manoeuvre without a demonstration of that complete movement.

Focus the search

For a requested variation, we could search over the relevant factors instead of searching the entire motion representation.

Our hypothesis: this structure improves generalization while preserving physical feasibility, with potential savings in demonstrations and computation.

Two routes to impact

Aerial motion learning.
Local research. Global markets.

01

NZ aerial robotics research

Hazardous tasks such as aerial tree pruning make flight demonstrations costly and risky.

Learning from fewer demonstrations and reducing inference costs could make new manoeuvres easier to develop and deploy on compact onboard computers.

02

The global RC hobby market

RC aerobatics take substantial practice, while crashes cost time and money.

Feasible motion generation could support shared-autonomy assistance for pilots, with commercial routes through controller licensing and training simulators.
